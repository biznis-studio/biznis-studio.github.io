# Client Retainer Renewal Kit

The moment a retainer or ongoing contract is coming up for renewal is
exactly when freelancers and consultants lose the most money quietly -
either by staying silent and hoping the client renews on their own, or by
renewing at the same rate for the third year running because raising it
felt awkward. This kit is two things that work together: ready-to-send
scripts for every renewal situation, and a simple tracker so no contract
end date ever sneaks up on you.

## How the two parts work together

- The scripts (this document) - the actual wording for eight specific
  renewal moments, from the first proactive check-in to ending a retainer
  gracefully.
- The Retainer Renewal Tracker (companion template) - one row per client:
  start date, end date, the date you should start the renewal
  conversation, current rate, and status. When a client's renewal-notice
  date arrives, that's your cue to open this document and pick the right
  script.

Used together: know the date is coming, reach out with the right words,
track where every client stands, never let a contract lapse by accident.

## Why this exists

Most advice about client relationships is about winning new clients, not
keeping and renewing the ones you already have - even though renewing an
existing client is almost always cheaper and faster than finding a new
one. The actual wording for these conversations is rarely written down
anywhere, so people either avoid the conversation or write it from
scratch, under time pressure, every single time.

## 1. The proactive check-in, well before the end date

Use this 30-60 days before a contract or retainer ends - reaching out
early is itself a signal of professionalism, and it gives both sides room
to actually negotiate instead of rushing a decision.

> Our current agreement runs through [end date], so I wanted to check in
> early. I'd love to continue working together - want to set up a short
> call in the next couple of weeks to talk through what the next period
> should look like?

## 2. Renewing at the same terms

Use this when everything is working well and neither side wants changes
- keep it short, since a long message here can accidentally introduce
doubt where there wasn't any.

> Glad to hear things are working well on your end. I'd like to propose
> renewing under the same terms for another [period] - same scope, same
> rate. If that works for you, I'll send over a quick renewal
> confirmation to sign.

## 3. Renewing with a rate increase

Use this when your rate needs to go up - state it plainly and early in
the message, then justify briefly rather than apologizing for it.

> As we look at renewing for the next [period], my rate is moving to
> [new rate], reflecting [brief, honest reason - increased scope, market
> rate, cost of doing business]. Everything else about how we work
> together stays the same. Happy to hop on a call if you'd like to talk
> it through.

## 4. The client wants to renew but shrink the budget

Use this when a client wants to continue but at less money - the goal is
to make the trade-off explicit rather than just quietly doing less work
for less pay without discussing what changes.

> I can absolutely work with a reduced budget for the next period - to do
> that well, here's what would need to adjust: [specific reduction in
> scope, hours, or deliverables that matches the new budget]. Want me to
> put together two options at different price points so you can pick
> what fits?

## 5. The client goes quiet as the renewal date approaches

Use this when you've reached out once already and haven't heard back,
and the end date is getting close - direct, without pressure or guilt.

> Following up as we're getting close to [end date] - just want to make
> sure we're aligned on next steps before then. Would love to keep
> working together; let me know if a quick call this week works, or if
> anything's changed on your end.

## 6. Converting a one-off project into an ongoing retainer

Use this after a successful project, when there's a natural opening to
propose ongoing work instead of waiting for the client to ask.

> Now that [the project] is wrapped up, I wanted to mention: if there's
> ongoing work in this area, I offer a retainer arrangement that could
> cover [specific ongoing need you noticed]. No pressure at all - just
> wanted to put it on your radar in case it's useful.

## 7. Ending a retainer gracefully, on your terms

Use this when you don't want to renew - clarity and enough notice matter
more here than justification.

> I wanted to give you plenty of notice: I won't be renewing our
> agreement after [end date]. It's been a genuine pleasure working
> together, and I'm happy to help with a smooth handoff of anything in
> progress before then.

## 8. A client who wants to "keep going" without formally renewing

Use this when a client keeps working with you past the end date without
ever signing anything new - common, and worth closing cleanly rather than
letting it drift indefinitely.

> I noticed we're now past our original end date of [date] without a
> formal renewal - since we're clearly continuing, let's get a quick
> updated agreement in place so we're both covered. I'll send something
> over this week; should take two minutes to sign.

## A short checklist for using these

- Set the renewal-notice date the moment a new contract starts, not when
  it's about to end - script 1 only works if you send it early
- Never let a rate increase be a surprise buried in a renewal email -
  script 3 states it in the first sentence, on purpose
- If a client goes quiet (script 5), send it once, then move on - don't
  chase a non-response repeatedly
- Decide your own minimum notice period for ending a retainer (script 7)
  before you're in the moment of actually needing to send it

## Where to go deeper

- If you use written contracts, check whether they already specify an
  auto-renewal or notice-period clause - these scripts work best
  alongside a clear contract, not as a replacement for one.
- Keeping a simple record of each client's original scope and rate makes
  script 3 (rate increases) and script 4 (budget reductions) much easier
  to write precisely when the moment comes.
