title: Scope creep by the numbers: what saying yes actually costs
description: A "small" unpaid request rarely feels like money leaving the room. Run the arithmetic once and it stops looking small.
---
Every freelancer knows the moment: the project is scoped, the contract is
signed, and then the message arrives. *"Quick thing - could you also just..."*
It is almost never presented as new work. It is presented as a favor, a
detail, a rounding error.

Here is the uncomfortable part: nobody tracks these. They do not show up on
any invoice, so they do not show up in any number you look at - which is
exactly why they keep happening.

## The arithmetic nobody runs

Take a deliberately modest example. Say your effective rate is $75/hour,
and a client's "small things" add up to just two extra hours a week -
one Slack request, one revision round that was not in scope.

- 2 hours × $75 = **$150 per week** of unbilled work
- Over a 6-month project: roughly **$3,900**
- If you run three client projects a year with the same pattern:
  **five figures** of work you did, and nobody ever agreed to pay for

The numbers are illustrative - your rate and your leak will differ. The
point is that the leak is *structural*, not accidental. It is the
predictable result of having no cheap way to say "yes, and that is billable"
in the moment.

## Why "just say no" advice fails

Browse [r/freelance](https://www.reddit.com/r/freelance/) or
[The Workplace Stack Exchange](https://workplace.stackexchange.com/) for an
evening and you will see the same story on repeat: it is never one big
overreach, it is ten small ones, and the freelancer stays quiet because the
wording feels confrontational. The hard part of scope creep is not noticing
it. It is finding words that hold the line *without* sounding difficult or
starting a fight over what was "obviously included."

That is a solvable problem, because the same handful of moments repeat on
every project:

- The first stretch-request, before it becomes a pattern
- The moment you need to name the pattern out loud
- The formal change request with time and cost attached
- The "but it's such a small thing" pushback
- The deadline that stopped being realistic because scope grew

## Treat it as a system, not a personality trait

Holding scope is not about becoming a tougher person. It is about having
two boring things ready before you need them: the wording for each of
those moments, and a log where every addition gets written down with an
estimate next to it. The log matters more than it sounds - a request that
gets logged with "+4 hours, $300" attached stops being a favor and starts
being a decision the client makes consciously.
