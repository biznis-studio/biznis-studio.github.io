title: How to tell a client you're raising your rates (email wording that works)
description: The exact email for a rate increase - what to include, what to leave out, how much notice to give, and what to say when a client pushes back.
---
Most freelancers undercharge for years for one reason: the email is
uncomfortable to write, so it never gets written. The work is worth more,
everyone involved knows it, and the rate stays where it was in year one
because nobody wants to be the one to bring it up.

Here is the wording. Use it directly or adapt it - the reasoning behind
each choice is explained, so you can write your own version.

## How to notify clients of a rate increase

Almost every version of this email that works has the same five parts, in
this order:

1. **The change, stated early.** Not buried under three paragraphs of
   appreciation. Burying it reads as guilt, and guilt invites
   negotiation.
2. **The new number and the date it starts.** Specific. "A small
   increase" is not a number.
3. **Notice long enough to be fair.** Thirty days is the practical floor
   for ongoing work; sixty is generous and almost never argued with.
4. **A brief reason - one sentence, not a defence.** Long justifications
   signal that you are unsure the increase is warranted.
5. **Continuity.** Say what is *not* changing. Most client anxiety about
   a price email is not the money, it is "is this relationship changing?"

Put together:

> Hi [name] - a quick note on pricing for next year. From [date], my rate
> for our ongoing work moves from [old] to [new]. That's the first change
> since we started working together in [year], and it brings me in line
> with what this kind of work costs now.
>
> Everything else stays exactly as it is - same scope, same turnaround,
> same way of working. Your invoices before [date] are unaffected.
>
> Happy to talk it through if it's useful. Otherwise no action needed
> from your side.

That last line does real work. "No action needed" gives the client
permission to simply accept, which most of them want to do. An email
ending in "let me know your thoughts" invites a negotiation you did not
have to open.

## How to announce a rate increase without apologising

The single most common mistake is apologising. "I'm so sorry to have to
do this", "unfortunately I've had to", "I hate to be the bearer of bad
news."

Every apology says the same thing: *I do not think this is justified.*
And if you do not think it is justified, the client will not either.

Replace apology with straightforwardness:

- Not "I'm sorry, but I need to raise my rates" → **"From March 1st, my
  rate moves to X."**
- Not "Unfortunately costs have gone up" → **"This is the first change in
  three years."**
- Not "I hope this is okay" → **"Happy to talk it through if useful."**

You are not asking permission. You are giving notice, professionally and
with time to plan - which is exactly what a supplier is supposed to do.

## How to politely tell someone your rate for new work

For a new client, the mechanics are different: there is no relationship
to protect and no history to justify. State it plainly, early, and attach
it to what they get:

> For a project of this scope, my rate is [X]. That covers [the two or
> three concrete things included]. If the budget you have in mind is
> different, tell me the number and I'll say honestly whether it's
> workable - I'd rather do that now than halfway through.

The offer to discuss budget openly is not weakness. It filters out
mismatches early, which is precisely when it costs least.

## What to say when a client pushes back

Some will. The reply that works acknowledges the constraint without
reopening the price:

> Completely understand - budgets are real. The rate from [date] reflects
> what the work involves now, so I'm not able to keep the old one going
> indefinitely. What I can do is look at the scope: if we adjust [X] or
> move to [less frequent arrangement], we can land closer to where you
> need to be. Would that be worth exploring?

The principle: **hold the rate, flex the scope.** Discounting the rate
teaches the client that your prices are opening offers. Reducing scope
keeps the value-per-hour intact and hands them a real choice.

## When to send it

- **Ongoing retainers:** at least 30 days ahead, ideally at a natural
  boundary - year end, contract anniversary, quarter start.
- **Project work:** at the quote, never after starting.
- **Never** in the same message as bad news, a delay, or an apology for
  something else. A rate increase attached to an apology reads as
  compensation for a failure.
