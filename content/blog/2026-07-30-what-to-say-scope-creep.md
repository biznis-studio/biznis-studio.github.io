The hard part of scope creep was never noticing it. You noticed the third
"quick favour" weeks ago. The hard part is the sentence you have to type
next - the one that says *this is extra* without sounding petty, rigid,
or like you are about to send a surprise invoice.

This page gives you that wording. Real, complete sentences you can copy,
adjust and send, plus the reasoning behind why each one works, so you can
write your own version when the situation does not match exactly.

## How to tell a client something is out of scope

The wording that works almost always does three things in this order:

1. **Say yes to the person before saying no to the request.** People hear
   "no" first and stop listening. Opening warmly buys you the next
   sentence.
2. **State it as a fact about the agreement, not a judgement about them.**
   "This falls outside what we scoped" is neutral. "You keep adding
   things" is an accusation, even when it is accurate.
3. **Offer a route forward, not a wall.** A client who hears only "no"
   has one option: argue. A client offered two paths picks one.

Here is that structure as a message you can actually send:

> Happy to help with this. Quick flag: this one falls a bit outside what
> we scoped originally. I can either fold it into the current timeline
> with a small extension, or quote it as a separate add-on - whichever
> works better for you. Let me know which you'd prefer and I'll get
> started.

Notice what is missing: no apology, no lengthy justification, and no
mention of money before the client has chosen a direction. Apologising
invites negotiation about whether the boundary is real.

## What to say the third or fourth time it happens

One request is a request. Four is a pattern, and the wording has to name
it - otherwise you are agreeing to a new normal by staying quiet.

> I want to flag something before it grows into a bigger issue: we've had
> a few additions over the last few weeks that sit outside the original
> scope - [name one or two, plainly and without editorialising].
> Individually they're small, but together they add real time. Can we
> take ten minutes to align on what's in scope from here, and how we
> handle new things as they come up?

The phrase doing the work is **"before it grows into a bigger issue"**.
It frames you as protecting the project rather than defending your
territory. Naming one or two specific examples matters too: vague
complaints get argued with, specifics get acknowledged.

## What to say when the client says "but it's such a small thing"

This one catches people out, because the client is often right that the
individual item is small. Arguing that it is actually big loses.

> Totally get that it feels small on its own - it's the accumulation on
> my side that adds up. I'd rather keep things clean and predictable for
> both of us than let small asks quietly turn into unpaid work. If it's
> important, let's approve it properly so it's tracked; if it can wait,
> we can bundle it into the next phase.

You concede the point and hold the boundary anyway. "For both of us" is
not filler - it reframes the process as mutual protection, which is
genuinely true: clients dislike surprise invoices as much as you dislike
unpaid work.

## A better way to say "scope creep"

Do not use the phrase with clients at all. It is industry jargon that
sounds like an accusation, and most clients have never heard it.

Say instead:

- "outside what we scoped"
- "a separate piece of work"
- "an addition to the original agreement"
- "a change request"

The last one is the most useful, because it sounds like a normal process
step rather than a complaint - which is exactly how you want additions to
feel.

## The part most people skip

Wording alone leaks. If additions are not written down somewhere with an
estimate attached, you end up in a conversation about memory - and by
invoice time nobody agrees on what was extra.

Keep a running list. Date, what was asked, who asked, rough hours, and
whether it was approved. It takes seconds per entry and it changes the
dynamic completely: a request logged as *"+4 hours, approved by
[name]"* stops being a favour and becomes a decision the client made
knowingly.
